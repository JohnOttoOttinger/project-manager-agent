In a post about the places I have worked, I mentioned a Mongolian yurt that was part of a puppetry centre in Glen Echo Park, Maryland, across the river from the CIA. I said there was probably another post in it. This is that post.

In 2004 I trained under Christopher Piper at The Puppet Co. in Glen Echo, learning to build and string marionettes. I was doing a graduate course in nonfiction writing at the same time, so I spent that year taking notes on the place and interviewing the man teaching me. Those pieces have been in a folder for twenty-two years. Everything below comes out of them.[/vc_column_text][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="40" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="40" screen_tablet_spacer_size="30" screen_mobile_spacer_size="38"][dfd_delimiter delimiter_style="dfd-delimiter-with-text" text_delimiter="Two Puppeteers and One Cinderella" delimiter_border_style="dotted" custom_fonts="font_family:Qwigley%3Aregular|font_style:400%20regular%3A400%3Anormal" module_animation="transition.expandIn" use_google_fonts="show" title_font_options="font_size:30|line_height:38"][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="15" screen_mobile_spacer_size="19"][vc_column_text css=""]

Piper started the company in 1983 with Allan Stevens. They had met at Glen Echo three years earlier, both working with puppets and both running their own companies. Stevens had won a grant for a new production of Cinderella and had no Cinderella. Piper had one.

“So we decided to join up,” he told me. They then moved into a group house with twenty-five other people, and twenty years later were still living under the same roof. Stevens does not drive. Stevens had run the Smithsonian Puppet Theater from 1969 to 1975. Piper is second-generation: his father Len ran a travelling puppet show out of Hawaii and Piper grew up on the road with it. “I lived in Hawaii for 17 years, but I’ve been to every state except Alaska. I broke my leg before I got there.” Their first playhouse opened in 1987, in an annex of the park’s dance hall that had been condemned before they got to it.[/vc_column_text][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="40" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="40" screen_tablet_spacer_size="30" screen_mobile_spacer_size="38"][dfd_delimiter delimiter_style="dfd-delimiter-with-text" text_delimiter="Five Hundred Shows a Year" delimiter_border_style="dotted" custom_fonts="font_family:Qwigley%3Aregular|font_style:400%20regular%3A400%3Anormal" module_animation="transition.expandIn" use_google_fonts="show" title_font_options="font_size:30|line_height:38"][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="15" screen_mobile_spacer_size="19"][vc_column_text css=""]

By 2004 the company was performing close to 500 shows a year and owned more than 300 puppets. “That’s the problem,” Piper said. “We’ve got a storage issue.” Two units out in Gaithersburg, both full.

What they perform is not entirely their decision. “Name recognition helps,” he said. “Years of Disney animation have pretty much decided what plays we’re going to produce.” There is no casting couch in puppetry, and being young is no advantage at the box office. The bankable names are made of felt, wire and wood, and they stay famous into their second and third centuries. Cinderella, Snow White, Rapunzel. Even a villain like Rumpelstiltskin keeps drawing children in.[/vc_column_text][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="40" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="40" screen_tablet_spacer_size="30" screen_mobile_spacer_size="38"][dfd_delimiter delimiter_style="dfd-delimiter-with-text" text_delimiter="The New Theatre" delimiter_border_style="dotted" custom_fonts="font_family:Qwigley%3Aregular|font_style:400%20regular%3A400%3Anormal" module_animation="transition.expandIn" use_google_fonts="show" title_font_options="font_size:30|line_height:38"][vc_single_image image="16354" img_size="full" add_caption="yes" alignment="center" style="vc_box_shadow_3d" css="" onclick="link_one_page" item_animation="transition.fadeIn" title="The Puppet Co. That February the company moved across the park walkway into the North Arcade, an Art Deco wing with neon signage. Six thousand square feet, 250 seats, two million dollars. The stage was twenty feet by twenty, with trap doors for hand puppets and a catwalk to fly things across. Some of the lights were hand-me-downs from a wealthier theatre up the road.

A week out it was a half-dressed shell that smelled of sawdust and paint, with lumber stacked down the hallways. Everything in it had been chosen for children, which means rounded edges, industrial-strength, or detachable and easy to replace. My notes describe the carpet as vomit-repellent. The box office glass was the kind you see in a late-night convenience store, which tells you something about selling tickets to a children’s show. A sign in the theatre read “Benches are for adults only. Priority basis.” Six days before opening, the curtain was still in a box in the basement. The first production in the new building was The Jungle Book, and what Piper wanted out of the move was size. “The productions we’ve done up to this point have been built so we could take them around to schools. Smaller puppets. Now we can build larger backgrounds and set pieces.” The sound system was muffled on the first night. “The warranty expired,” he said. “But what can you do?”[/vc_column_text][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="40" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="40" screen_tablet_spacer_size="30" screen_mobile_spacer_size="38"][dfd_delimiter delimiter_style="dfd-delimiter-with-text" text_delimiter="To the Yurt We Go" delimiter_border_style="dotted" custom_fonts="font_family:Qwigley%3Aregular|font_style:400%20regular%3A400%3Anormal" module_animation="transition.expandIn" use_google_fonts="show" title_font_options="font_size:30|line_height:38"][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="15" screen_mobile_spacer_size="19"][vc_column_text css=""]

The yurt had been bought from the Smithsonian when a Mongolia exhibit closed, about twenty years earlier, and put up in the park by volunteers. It was the company’s workshop, and it was where the puppets went when a show finished. Most of the cast hung from hooks or lay folded into impossible positions in boxes marked Nutcracker and Oz.

An hour after the last show of the weekend, it had to be emptied. Piper is not a large man and he visibly went out at the thought of an afternoon of work that was not creative. Then he straightened up and announced, “To the yurt we go!” He spent the afternoon under the new stage, standing on scaffolding in the pit while volunteers passed puppets down through the trap doors. Rapunzel, Cinderella, the Three Little Pigs. He had not seen some of them in years. His favourite came down in a clear bag with its nose pressed against the plastic, a Pinocchio that Stevens had sculpted and wired. I had asked him about it earlier. “The way the face looks, the way it moves,” he said. “He has a few favorites of mine, too. We’ve been working together for so long. It goes both ways.” Then a red wooden box came down through the floor, dusty, hand-lettered in gold along the side: Len Piper Puppet Theater, Hawaii. Piper looked at it the way you would look at something holy. He picked two puppets out of the bagged audience at his feet and started loading his father’s old trunk. “There you go.”[/vc_column_text][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="40" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="40" screen_tablet_spacer_size="30" screen_mobile_spacer_size="38"][dfd_delimiter delimiter_style="dfd-delimiter-with-text" text_delimiter="What I Took From It" delimiter_border_style="dotted" custom_fonts="font_family:Qwigley%3Aregular|font_style:400%20regular%3A400%3Anormal" module_animation="transition.expandIn" use_google_fonts="show" title_font_options="font_size:30|line_height:38"][vc_single_image image="16349" img_size="full" add_caption="yes" alignment="center" style="vc_box_shadow_3d" css="" onclick="link_one_page" item_animation="transition.fadeIn" title="Where the strings attach decides what the puppet can do"][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="15" screen_mobile_spacer_size="19"][vc_column_text css=""]

What I learned there was building and stringing marionettes, and stringing is the part nobody watches. Where each string attaches decides what the puppet is able to do. A head hung a centimetre out will never look alive, whoever is working it, and you find that out by getting it wrong and doing it again.

That has been the useful part ever since. Everything I have made since is the same problem at a different size. An oversized head carried on a pole through a street, a costume with somebody inside it, a character worked live through a projector. They are all objects a person has to operate, and the ones that fail are the ones where that was worked out after the drawing was finished. Piper was in his fifties when I met him and had been doing it since he could walk. I got one year of it. It was the most useful apprenticeship I have had.[/vc_column_text][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="40" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="40" screen_tablet_spacer_size="30" screen_mobile_spacer_size="38"][vc_hoverbox image="16348" primary_title="Puppet Design" hover_title="Puppet Designer" hover_title_use_theme_fonts="yes" hover_title_css_animation="none" hover_background_color="custom" use_custom_fonts_hover_title="true" hover_custom_background="rgba(97,124,62,0.38)" hover_title_link="url:https%3A%2F%2Fwww.oddtoe.com%2Fartist-designer%2Fpuppet-designer%2F|title:Puppet%20Designer"]Marionettes, rod and carried puppets, oversized heads and shadow work — designed in 3D and drawn for a maker to build.[/vc_hoverbox][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="10" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="10" screen_tablet_spacer_size="10" screen_mobile_spacer_size="5"][vc_column_text css=""]
