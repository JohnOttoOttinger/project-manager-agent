What decides how a microsite gets built?

[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="20" screen_mobile_spacer_size="20"][vc_column_text css=""]

A microsite’s structure follows its purpose. A single campaign page might be one long scroll; an annual report usually needs several linked sections — highlights, financials, a downloadable summary. Page count and data complexity are what move a project from a short build to a larger one.

We start every microsite the same way we start a dashboard: by asking what the reader needs to leave understanding. A microsite that tries to hold everything ends up holding nothing — the ones that work pick one job and do it well, whether that is a single campaign page or a full report with a dozen linked sections.

A microsite is a small, standalone site or page built around one purpose — a campaign, a piece of research, or an interactive annual report — instead of one more page inside your main website’s navigation. It usually has its own short reading path from start to finish, rather than an open-ended menu structure.

[/vc_column_text][/vc_tta_section][vc_tta_section title="How is a microsite different from a normal website page?" tab_id="1685677784323-438be9dd-da0e6fce-87a69a68-2eae"][vc_column_text css=""]

A normal page sits inside your site’s menu and competes with everything else on it. By contrast, a microsite is built to be read start to finish, is usually shared as its own link, and can be retired once the campaign, report, or project it supports has run its course.

What actually goes into designing a microsite?

[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="15" screen_mobile_spacer_size="19"][vc_column_text css=""]

I treat a microsite brief the same way I treat a dashboard brief: what does the reader need to understand, and what is the one thing they should do next? Before any design starts, I want the content — the report, the research, or the campaign copy — even in rough form, because the structure of a microsite comes from what it has to hold, not the other way around.

Once I have the content, I map it into sections a reader can scroll through in order, then design each one the way I would design a dashboard panel — one clear point, supported by the smallest amount of chart, image, or copy that makes it. A microsite that reads like a brochure has usually skipped this step and started with a template instead of the content.

For an interactive annual report, that means turning tables of figures into charts a reader can actually compare, and giving the whole document a table of contents that behaves like a real website when a reader clicks through it. The same discipline applies to a smaller brief — a campaign page still needs a clear reading order, even if it is three sections instead of ten.

The build itself lives on your own website, using the same data visualisation and dashboard design tools I use for corporate clients, styled to match your brand — not a separate platform with its own login. If your team would rather build this skill in-house, our workshops cover the same design thinking; otherwise every quote starts with a look at what you already have.

How does microsite design fit with dashboards and style guides?

[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="15" screen_mobile_spacer_size="19"][vc_column_text css=""]

In practice, a microsite is not a replacement for the rest of what I do — it usually sits next to it. Most of the clients who ask about a microsite already have a dashboard or a BI style guide from us, and the same visual language carries across: the same chart types, the same colour rules, the same typographic choices, so the microsite reads as part of one system.

That consistency matters more for an interactive annual report than almost anything else I build, because it is the one document that has to represent the whole organisation at once. See our data visualisation style guides page if a style guide is the piece you are missing before a microsite makes sense.

For teams already using Power BI or Tableau internally, a microsite is often the public-facing companion to a dashboard nobody outside the company ever sees — the same figures, rebuilt for an audience that will never open the underlying file. Our Power BI dashboard design work and our microsite work draw on the same design decisions, for two different audiences.

I started building these kinds of pages after learning visual storytelling at National Geographic, long before “microsite” was a common word for it. What has not changed is the test I still use: if you took the microsite away, would the reader be missing something a normal web page could not have told them? If yes, it is worth building properly.
