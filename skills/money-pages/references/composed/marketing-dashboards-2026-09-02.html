How is a custom dashboard different from a template?

[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="20" screen_mobile_spacer_size="20"][vc_column_text css=""]

A template ships with someone else’s chart choices and default metrics. A custom marketing dashboard starts from your campaigns, channels, and reporting cadence, so every chart on the page answers the question your team actually asks each week.

Templates are a reasonable starting point for a small team moving off spreadsheets — our Power BI templates exist for exactly that. A template cannot know that your team tracks cost per lead by channel, or that leadership only cares about three numbers out of a twelve-tab report. A custom-built dashboard is scoped around your actual KPIs and your actual data sources, built to the metrics your team already reports on. That difference matters most once a team outgrows the basics: more campaigns, more channels, and a report that used to take an afternoon to assemble by hand.

How is this different from a general BI dashboard?

[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="20" screen_mobile_spacer_size="20"][vc_column_text css=""]

Marketing gets its own dashboard because its metrics change weekly: campaign, traffic, and spend numbers your team actually reports on. A general BI dashboard usually mixes finance, operations, and sales metrics that only a few people ever open.

Scoping a dashboard to one function is a design decision. A dashboard trying to serve marketing, finance, and operations at once tends to serve none of them well — too many metrics compete for the same screen, and nobody can find the one number they actually track that week. Building a marketing-specific view means every chart on the page answers a question a marketer actually asks.

How we design a marketing dashboard

[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="15" screen_mobile_spacer_size="19"][vc_row_inner][vc_column_inner width="1/2"][vc_column_text css=""]

I start every marketing dashboard project with a short conversation, not a data pull. What does your team actually decide with this data each week? Which campaigns get paused, which channels get more budget, which numbers go in the Monday report? Those answers decide the layout long before I open Power BI or Tableau.

Most marketing teams already track plenty of numbers — impressions, clicks, spend, conversions, cost per lead — spread across five or six platforms. The dashboard’s job is not to show every metric available. It puts the handful your team actually acts on in one place, arranged so the story is obvious at a glance instead of buried in a table.

I sketch the layout before touching colour or chart type. A campaign-comparison view, a spend-versus-budget tracker, and a channel breakdown usually need three different chart shapes, instead of one dashboard template stretched to cover all three. Getting the layout right first is what keeps the finished dashboard from becoming another report nobody opens.

[/vc_column_text][/vc_column_inner][vc_column_inner width="1/2"][vc_column_text css=""]

Once the layout is agreed, the build happens in whichever tool your data already lives in. If your team already reports out of Power BI, that is where the dashboard lives too — see our Power BI dashboard design work for examples. Tableau and Excel builds follow the same process.

The dashboards that get used long after the project ends are the ones that answer a real question every time someone opens them. That is the target for every build here: a tool your team opens on a Monday morning, never a showcase piece.

I founded The Datalabs Agency in 2012, in Melbourne, after learning visual storytelling at National Geographic. That background shapes how I still think about a marketing dashboard: it is a piece of visual communication first, and a data connection second.

Since then I have built dashboards and BI style guides for clients including Mercedes-Benz, Adidas, and UPS, alongside corporate training workshops in Power BI, Tableau — see our Tableau dashboard design work — and data storytelling. I still get involved directly in design decisions on every project. Dashboard work, especially the marketing kind, rewards someone who has sat through enough campaign reviews to know which number actually changes a decision and which one just fills a slide.
