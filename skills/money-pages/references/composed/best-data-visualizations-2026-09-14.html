What makes the best data visualizations?

[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="20" screen_mobile_spacer_size="20"][vc_column_text css=""]

The best data visualizations pick a chart type that fits the exact comparison being made, use one accent colour to carry the point, trim axes and labels to only what the reader needs, and state the takeaway instead of leaving the reader to find it. Volume of data does not make a visualization good — clarity does.

The Datalabs Agency is a Melbourne-based data visualization consultancy founded in 2012 that delivers corporate training workshops (Power BI, Tableau, data storytelling), dashboard design, and BI style guides for clients including Mercedes-Benz, Adidas, and UPS.

Should you hire a designer?

[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="20" screen_mobile_spacer_size="20"][vc_column_text css=""]

For a one-off chart in a slide, no — a template is enough. For a dashboard your team checks weekly, an annual report investors read, or anything carrying your brand and your data, a trained designer earns the fee by making the same numbers easier to act on.

Software does not make this decision for you. Power BI and Tableau can both produce a dashboard that is hard to read at a glance, or one a manager can scan in ten seconds — the difference sits in choices about hierarchy, colour, and what to leave off the page, not in which product built it. A designer who has made that call hundreds of times makes it faster and more consistently than a team member fitting it in between other work.

That is also where cost shows up honestly. A DIY dashboard costs nothing in dollars and a great deal in the hours it takes to get right, revise, and eventually rebuild once the format stops working. A commissioned dashboard or style guide costs a fixed, quoted fee up front and comes with a design system your team can reuse without starting from a blank canvas each time.

What ruins good visualizations?

[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="20" screen_mobile_spacer_size="20"][vc_column_text css=""]

A single overloaded chart is the most common failure — too many series, too many colours, no stated point. Misleading scales, a chart type that does not match the comparison, and unlabelled axes come next, each easy to miss in review and large enough to change what a reader takes away.

Chart type is the mistake I see most often, because most tools default to whichever chart you used last time instead of the one the data actually calls for. Our guide to data visualization types walks through which chart fits which kind of comparison, and it is worth checking before you build the next one.

What a template can't fix

[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="20" screen_mobile_spacer_size="20"][vc_column_text css=""]

A template supplies the shell — the fonts, the colours, the grid. It does not decide which three numbers matter most on a crowded dashboard, or catch a chart type that quietly misleads. That judgement is what a workshop teaches your team, or what a designer supplies project by project.

Every dashboard we design starts with the same question: what does this need to prove, and to whom? A finance dashboard for a CFO and a marketing dashboard for a campaign team can draw on the same data warehouse and still need completely different layouts, because the two readers are looking for different things in the first five seconds. Software will not ask that question for you — a person still has to.

What separates a good visualization from a great one

[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="15" screen_mobile_spacer_size="19"][vc_row_inner][vc_column_inner width="1/2"][vc_column_text css=""]

I have looked at thousands of charts, dashboards, and reports since I started in visual storytelling at National Geographic in the early 2000s, and the good ones share a habit the average ones skip: they decide what the reader should notice before a single pixel gets drawn.

Most charts start from the data and let the shape fall out however it falls. The stronger approach starts from the one sentence you want the reader to walk away with, then picks the chart type, colour, and labelling that makes that sentence obvious at a glance. A bar chart sorted by value tells a different story than the same bars left in alphabetical order, even though the underlying numbers have not changed at all.

Colour is where I see the most waste. A chart with ten categories in ten colours asks the reader to do the sorting the designer should have done. One accent colour on the bar or line that matters, with everything else in a neutral grey, carries the same point with a fraction of the effort.

[/vc_column_text][/vc_column_inner][vc_column_inner width="1/2"][vc_column_text css=""]

None of this depends much on the software. Power BI and Tableau can both produce a cluttered dashboard or a clear one; the difference sits in the choices made before the first chart goes on the canvas. See our workshop pricing if your team wants to learn these choices directly instead of outsourcing them project by project.

The visualizations I remember are rarely the busiest ones on the page. They are the ones where I could describe the point in one sentence without looking back at the chart.

How we design a visualization that holds up

[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="15" screen_mobile_spacer_size="19"][vc_row_inner][vc_column_inner width="1/2"][vc_column_text css=""]

When a client asks us to design a dashboard, the first meeting is rarely about software. We ask who looks at it, how often, and what decision it is meant to support — a dashboard a CFO checks weekly needs a different layout than one a marketing team pulls up mid-meeting, even when both draw from the same data warehouse.

From there we sketch the hierarchy before we touch a chart type: what is the one number that matters most, what are the two or three that give it context, and what can be cut entirely. Most of the clutter in a first-draft dashboard is data that is technically relevant and practically distracting.

We are tool-agnostic by design — the same judgement about hierarchy, colour, and labelling applies whether a team lives in Power BI, Tableau, or a custom web build. For interactive annual reports specifically, we use interactive chart generation, web animations, and interactive diagrams, so the report gets edited when the numbers change instead of redesigned from scratch each year.

[/vc_column_text][/vc_column_inner][vc_column_inner width="1/2"][vc_column_text css=""]

Once the layout is right, we build a short style guide alongside it — colour roles, chart-type rules, spacing — so the next dashboard a client's team builds without us still matches it.
