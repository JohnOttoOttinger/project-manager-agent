<p style="text-align: center;">What does a character design service actually do?</p>
<p>[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="20" screen_mobile_spacer_size="20"][vc_column_text css=""]</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;"><strong>Oddtoe</strong> turns a brief, a script or a mood board into an original character ready for production, designing the <strong>look, personality and documentation</strong> together in <strong>2D, 2.5D or 3D</strong>, so a studio, agency or games team can hand the character to other artists and get a consistent result. The practice has a long pedigree: <strong>Oddtoe</strong> spent years designing <strong><a class="dfd-custom-link-decorated" href="/national-geographic-kids-case-study/">characters and games for National Geographic Kids</a></strong>.</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;"><strong>Oddtoe</strong> is an experiential design and generative-AI animation studio based in Melbourne, creating projection, installation, and animated work for events, venues, and galleries.</p>
<p>[/vc_column_text][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="80" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="80" screen_tablet_spacer_size="80" screen_mobile_spacer_size="80"][dfd_heading enable_delimiter="" style="style_02" heading_margin="margin-bottom:20px;" subtitle="Two words, two different jobs" title_font_options="tag:h2|font_size:70|line_height:64" subtitle_font_options="tag:h3|font_size:38|line_height:32"]</p>
<p style="text-align: center;">Character designer or character design service, and does the difference matter?</p>
<p>[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="20" screen_mobile_spacer_size="20"][vc_column_text css=""]</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">One character designer draws in <strong>their own style</strong>, at whatever pace they have free. A studio built for character design scopes the brief, <strong>documents the character properly</strong> and stays with the project through revisions, so the style does not drift between the first sketch and the hundredth frame.</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;"><p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">The distinction matters most once a character has to survive <strong>more than one artist touching it</strong>. A single illustrator can hold a look in their head. The moment a second animator, a games team or an ad agency picks up the same character, someone needs a <strong>spec sheet</strong>: turnarounds, expressions, proportions, the details that keep a character on-model instead of drifting a little with every new hand.</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">It matters again on <strong>format</strong>. A character built for a static illustration is not automatically ready for a 3D model or an animated turn. <strong>Oddtoe</strong> designs with the eventual use in mind, so a character can move from 2D concept art to a 2.5D shaded pass to a full <strong>3D model</strong> without a second designer reverse-engineering the first one's choices.</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">It cuts both ways: for a single static illustration in a style one artist already owns, a freelancer is often the better buy. See the <strong><a class="dfd-custom-link-decorated" href="https://www.oddtoe.com/artist-designer/character-designer/">character designer portfolio</a></strong> for the range of styles <strong>Oddtoe</strong> works in.</p></p>

<p style="text-align: center;">It depends on how many characters the brief needs, how many <strong>formats</strong> (2D, 2.5D or 3D) and how much <strong>documentation</strong> goes with each one. <strong>Oddtoe</strong> quotes per project after a short scoping conversation, so the number reflects your brief rather than a rate card you have to reverse-engineer.</p>
<p>[/vc_column_text][/vc_tta_section][vc_tta_section title="How do I choose a character design service?" tab_id="1685677784323-438be9dd-da0e6fce-87a69a68-2eae"][vc_column_text css=""]</p>
<p style="text-align: center;">Look at three things: whether the <strong>portfolio shows characters</strong>, not general illustration, whether <strong>one designer stays with the project</strong> through revisions, and whether they hand over proper <strong>spec sheets and turnarounds</strong> so other artists can draw the character consistently.</p>
<p>[/vc_column_text][/vc_tta_section][vc_tta_section title="What formats does a character design service deliver?" tab_id="1685677784395-526dd6ff-b9c26fce-87a69a68-2eae"][vc_column_text css=""]</p>

Most briefs also get a spec sheet covering expressions, turnarounds and proportions, so the character stays consistent once other hands are drawing it.</p>

<p style="text-align: center;">What can a character design service create for you?</p>

<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;"><strong>2D illustration</strong>, <strong>2.5D</strong> shaded designs, and full <strong>3D models</strong> built for animation or games, each backed by a spec sheet covering expressions, turnarounds and proportions so the character stays consistent once other artists are drawing it.</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;"><p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">Characters rarely arrive finished in one sitting. <strong>Oddtoe</strong> usually starts from a <strong>mood board or script</strong> and works through rough sketches before settling a final design, the same process behind the <strong><a class="dfd-custom-link-decorated" href="https://www.oddtoe.com/artist-designer/character-designer/">character designer portfolio</a></strong>.</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">Once a character is designed and documented, the next question is usually <strong>what it does next</strong>. Some go straight into motion &mdash; see the <strong><a class="dfd-custom-link-decorated" href="https://www.oddtoe.com/studio/generative-ai-animator/">generative AI animator</a></strong> page for how <strong>Oddtoe</strong> treats AI as one technique in that pipeline. When that hand-off goes to an outside team, the shortlist is usually <strong><a class="dfd-custom-link-decorated" href="https://www.oddtoe.com/ai-animation-studios/">AI animation studios</a></strong>, and the same directed-versus-generated question decides it. Others need a <strong>voice and a story</strong> first, which is where <strong><a class="dfd-custom-link-decorated" href="https://www.oddtoe.com/artist-designer/comedy-writer/">comedy and story writing</a></strong> comes in.</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">Characters that need to exist <strong>off the screen</strong>, as a costume, a prop or a physical build for an event, go through <strong><a class="dfd-custom-link-decorated" href="https://www.oddtoe.com/artist-designer/prop-designer-maker/">prop and 3D making</a></strong> instead, using the same design as its starting point.</p></p>

<p style="text-align: center;">How do you choose a character design service?</p>
<p>[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="20" screen_mobile_spacer_size="20"][vc_column_text css=""]</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;"><strong>Three tests.</strong> Does the portfolio show <strong>characters</strong>, not general illustration, will <strong>one designer stay with the project</strong> through revisions, and do they hand over proper <strong>spec sheets</strong> so other artists can draw the character consistently?</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;"><p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">The portfolio test catches the most common mismatch. Plenty of illustrators can draw a striking single image; far fewer have designed a character built to be <strong>redrawn hundreds of times</strong> by other people without losing what made it work.</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">The continuity test matters because a character usually outlives the <strong>first design pass</strong>. If the designer who understood the brief is not the one answering revision notes in week four, you end up <strong>re-explaining the character</strong> to whoever inherited it.</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">The documentation test is the one people skip until it costs them. A character with no turnaround sheet looks fine in the first render and <strong>drifts</strong> in the second. I would rather hand over a spec sheet nobody needs than leave a studio guessing at an angle nobody drew.</p></p>

<p style="text-align: center;">One designer, one style, start to finish</p>
<p>[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="15" screen_mobile_spacer_size="19"][vc_row_inner][vc_column_inner width="1/2"][vc_column_text]<br />
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">The case for hiring separate freelancers per asset is that it can be <strong>cheaper</strong> per image, and on a single static piece it often is. What that plan leaves out is what happens when the character has to work a second time: a different pose, a different medium, a sequel spot six months later. Somebody has to reverse-engineer the original designer's choices, and a look that took one afternoon to draw can take a week to <strong>match</strong>.</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">That is the part I try to remove up front. I take the brief, design the character in whichever formats the project actually needs, and build the <strong>documentation</strong> alongside it, not after somebody asks for it. A finished spec sheet means the next artist who touches the character, whether that is an animator, a games studio or a licensing partner, is working from the same reference I was.</p>[/vc_column_text][/vc_column_inner][vc_column_inner width="1/2"][vc_column_text]<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">The second thing a dedicated character design service buys is <strong>range across formats</strong>. A <strong>2D illustration</strong> and a <strong>3D model</strong> of the same character are different disciplines, and a character designed only for a still image often needs real rework before it can turn in three dimensions. Designing with the eventual use in mind from the first sketch avoids most of that rework.</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">None of that makes a full service the right answer <strong>every time</strong>. A single illustration in a style one artist already owns does not need a spec sheet built for a production it will never enter. The rule I use is simple: <strong>the more hands will touch the character</strong>, and the longer it has to last, the more the documentation is worth paying for up front.</p><br />[/vc_column_text][/vc_column_inner][/vc_row_inner][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="80" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="80" screen_tablet_spacer_size="60" screen_mobile_spacer_size="76"][/vc_column][vc_column width="1/6"][/vc_column][/vc_row][vc_row][vc_column][vc_column_text]<br />

<p style="text-align: center;">How to brief a character design service</p>
<p>[/dfd_heading][dfd_spacer screen_wide_resolution="1280" screen_wide_spacer_size="20" screen_normal_resolution="1024" screen_tablet_resolution="800" screen_mobile_resolution="480" screen_normal_spacer_size="20" screen_tablet_spacer_size="15" screen_mobile_spacer_size="19"][vc_row_inner][vc_column_inner width="1/2"][vc_column_text]<br />
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">Tell me <strong>who the character is</strong>, not just what they look like. Personality, role in the story, and one or two reference characters for the tone you are after go further than a shot list. A rough brief is fine; working out the physical detail from a loose description is part of what you are paying for.</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">Three things change a quote more than anything else: how many <strong>formats</strong> the character needs (2D, 2.5D, 3D), how much <strong>documentation</strong> has to go with it, and whether it is one character or a cast. One 2D illustration and a fully rigged 3D model with a turnaround sheet are different jobs even at the same brief length.</p>[/vc_column_text][/vc_column_inner][vc_column_inner width="1/2"][vc_column_text]<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;"><strong>Say what you already have.</strong> A script, a brand guideline, an existing character bible, or a previous design you did not like all <strong>shorten the scoping conversation</strong>. Saying which influences you are drawn to, or which you want to avoid, does the same.</p>
<p style="line-height: 22px; text-align: left;">If you already know the character needs to <strong>move</strong>, say that too, since it changes how the design is built from the first sketch.
